# What a good SaaS hero section actually looks like

*LinkedIn Article — Humbl Design | David Pokorny*

---

At Humbl Design, I audit SaaS landing pages every week. The hero section kills more signups than any other part of the page. And almost every time, it's the same handful of mistakes.

The layout is usually fine. The font is fine. The spacing is fine.

The problem is the words and the order.

---

## The hero has one job

A visitor lands on your page. They give you roughly 3 seconds to answer three questions:

What is this? Who is it for? Why should I care?

If the hero doesn't answer all three fast, they leave. They don't scroll to find out more. They don't read your feature section. They're gone.

[Research published in Behaviour & Information Technology](https://doi.org/10.1080/01449290500330448) found that users form a visual impression of a webpage in as little as 50 milliseconds. [Nielsen Norman Group's reading studies](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/) show users read less than 20% of text on a given page. Your hero is doing almost all of the work, on a timer you can't see.

It has to work immediately.

---

## The 4 elements that have to work together

**1. The headline**

Most SaaS headlines describe the product. "The all-in-one platform for modern teams." "AI-powered collaboration for growing companies." These tell me what you built. Nothing about what changes in my life after I buy.

The best headlines are specific outcomes or sharp problems. Linear's headline gets straight to it. Vercel's does too. Neither wastes the headline on a category claim.

Two patterns that convert:

*Outcome-led:* "Your team ships twice a week. Ours gets you there."
*Problem-led:* "Your engineers waste 3 hours a day on status updates."

Both make the reader feel seen. A category description makes no one feel anything.

**2. The subheadline**

The subheadline's job is to answer the headline. It adds two things: who this is for, and how it works.

Most subheadlines fail both. They restate the headline in different words ("A powerful platform that helps teams collaborate more effectively") or pile on so many use cases they mean nothing.

A subheadline that works: "Built for engineering teams at seed stage. Connects to Linear, Jira, or Notion in under 3 minutes."

Specific audience. Specific mechanism. Done.

**3. The CTA**

One CTA above the fold. One.

Two competing buttons ("Start free" and "Book a demo" side by side) make the visitor pick. Picking requires thinking. Thinking creates friction. Friction kills conversions.

Pick your primary action based on your conversion goal right now. If you're building the list, "Start free trial" is your CTA. If you're selling high-ticket B2B, "Book a demo" is your CTA. Put the other option in the nav or lower on the page.

Copy matters more than most founders realize. "Get started" is the weakest CTA on the internet. It says nothing about what happens when I click.

Three versions of the same CTA, different results:

- "Get started" → ambiguous, no urgency
- "Start free — no card required" → clear, kills the main objection before it forms
- "Get your free audit" → tells me exactly what I receive

When you name what happens after the click, more people click.

**4. Social proof**

This one gets skipped most often by early-stage companies. The thinking: we don't have 200 customer logos yet, so we have nothing to put here.

Wrong.

Social proof is any signal that reduces the risk of trusting you. Logos are one version of it. A number works just as well ("47 teams already on the waitlist"). A quote from your first user works. A mention from a recognizable media outlet works. Even "built by ex-Stripe engineers" is a trust signal.

And placement matters as much as what you say. Proof sitting on a dedicated Testimonials page in the nav is decoration. Put it where hesitation lives: right next to the CTA. If someone is almost ready to click and they see "Trusted by 130+ seed-stage teams," that's the thing that turns almost-decided into decided.

## The 5 ways founders kill it

**Feature-first headlines.** "AI-powered workflow automation" describes the product, not the outcome. If your headline sounds like a category, rewrite it around what the user's life looks like after they buy.

**Subheadlines that restate the headline.** If the headline says "ship faster" and the subheadline says "our platform helps your team move quickly," you've said the same thing twice. The subheadline should add information, not echo.

**Stock visuals.** The team high-fiving. The abstract purple gradient blob. The 3D floating laptop. None of these tell me what the product does. The best hero visuals show the product itself or show the outcome. A dashboard screenshot. A real result. Something I can actually look at.

**Two CTAs fighting each other.** Pick one and commit. Put the secondary action somewhere that isn't competing for the same attention.

**No social proof above the fold.** Even one line. Even a number. Something that tells the visitor: other people tried this and it worked.

## What a fixed hero looks like

**Before:**

Headline: *Streamline your team's workflow with AI-powered tools*
Subheadline: *Our platform helps modern teams collaborate better and work smarter*
CTA: *Get started*
Social proof: none above the fold

**After:**

Headline: *Your engineers are losing 3 hours a day to status updates*
Subheadline: *[Product] fixes it. Seed-stage SaaS teams connect in 2 minutes. No process overhaul required.*
CTA: *Start free — no card required*
Social proof: *Trusted by 130+ engineering teams* / [3 company logos]

Same product. Same page. Completely different first impression.

The after version answers all three questions in under 5 seconds. What is this (fixes status update overhead). Who is it for (seed-stage SaaS teams). Why should I care (3 hours a day is a lot to lose).

---

## How to check yours right now

Open your landing page. Cover the logo. Give it to someone who doesn't know your product and ask them to explain it back to you in one sentence.

If they can't, your hero is broken.

If they can but they describe the feature instead of the outcome, your headline is broken.

If they get it but they're not sure who it's for, your subheadline needs work.

That's the audit. It takes 5 minutes. Most founders skip it.
