## Article content

At Humbl Design, I've audited landing pages for 50+ early-stage founders. The hero section is responsible for more lost signups than any other element on the page. Not because of bad design — the layouts are usually fine. The problem is almost always the words and the order.

A visitor lands on your page with one question: "is this for me?" If the hero doesn't answer that in 3 seconds, they're gone. They don't scroll down to find out more. They don't read your features section. They close the tab.

[Research published in Behaviour & Information Technology](https://doi.org/10.1080/01449290500330448) found that users form a first visual impression in as little as 50 milliseconds. [Nielsen Norman Group's reading studies](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/) show users read less than 20% of the text on a page. Your hero carries the full weight of that first impression, on a timer you can't see.

This is the 4-element framework I use in every audit.

## The 4 elements every SaaS hero needs

### 1. A headline that leads with outcome, not feature

Most SaaS headlines describe the product. "The all-in-one platform for modern teams." "AI-powered collaboration for growing companies." These are category descriptions, not value propositions. They tell me what you built. Nothing about what changes in my life after I buy.

The best headlines lead with a specific outcome or a sharp problem. Here are the two patterns that consistently work:

**Outcome-led:** What does the user's world look like after using your product?
- "Your team ships twice a week. Ours gets you there."
- "Reports that used to take Monday morning now take 10 minutes."

**Problem-led:** What's the pain you remove?
- "Your engineers waste 3 hours a day on status updates."
- "Your trial signups aren't converting. Here's why."

Both make the reader feel seen. A category description makes no one feel anything.

The simplest test: remove your logo and show your homepage to someone who doesn't know your product. Ask them to explain what you do in one sentence. If they describe a feature instead of an outcome, your headline is broken. This is the same test I run in every [Humbl Audit](https://humbldesign.io).

### 2. A subheadline that adds, not echoes

The subheadline has one job: answer the headline with two things the headline can't hold — who this is for, and how it works.

Most subheadlines fail both. They restate the headline in slightly different words ("A powerful platform that helps modern teams collaborate more effectively") or try to speak to every possible use case at once, which means speaking to no one.

A subheadline that works is specific:

"Built for engineering teams at seed stage. Connects to Linear, Jira, or Notion in under 3 minutes."

Two pieces of information. Specific audience. Specific mechanism. Done.

If your subheadline could appear on a competitor's page unchanged, it's not doing its job.

### 3. One CTA, with specific copy

One call-to-action above the fold. One.

Two competing buttons — "Start free" and "Book a demo" placed side by side — force the visitor to pick. Picking requires thinking. Thinking creates friction. And friction, at that stage of the page, kills conversions. This is one of the most common pieces of [UX friction that kills conversions](https://humbldesign.io/blog-posts/saas-ux-mistakes-killing-mrr) I see across SaaS landing pages.

Choose your primary action based on your conversion goal right now. Building the list? "Start free trial" is your CTA. Selling high-ticket B2B where you need a qualified conversation? "Book a demo" is your CTA. Put the secondary option in the nav or below the fold. Give it space, just not the same space.

Copy matters more than most founders realize. "Get started" is the weakest CTA on the internet. It says nothing about what happens after the click.

Compare these three versions:
- "Get started" — ambiguous, no urgency
- "Start free — no card required" — clear, removes the main objection before it forms
- "Get your free audit" — names exactly what the user receives

When you name what happens after the click, more people click. The specificity is the reason.

### 4. Social proof, placed next to the decision

Social proof is any signal that reduces the risk of trusting you. Most early-stage founders skip this section because they think they need 50 customer logos before it applies to them. They don't.

Logos are one version of social proof. A number works just as well: "47 teams already on the waitlist." A quote from your first user works. A press mention works. Even "built by ex-Stripe engineers" reduces risk for the right reader.

What matters most is placement. Social proof sitting on a dedicated Testimonials page in the nav is decoration — it's too far from any real decision. Put it as close to your CTA as possible. If someone is almost ready to click and they see "Trusted by 130+ seed-stage teams," that's often the signal that converts almost-decided into decided.

For a deeper breakdown of how to build and place proof across your whole site, read this post on [turning social proof into a conversion asset](https://humbldesign.io/blog-posts/customer-proof-design).

## The 5 most common ways founders kill their hero

**Feature-first headlines.** "AI-powered workflow automation" is a category description, not a value proposition. If your headline sounds like something you'd write on a spec sheet, rewrite it around the outcome.

**Subheadlines that repeat the headline.** If the headline says "ship faster" and the subheadline says "our platform helps your team move quickly," you've used two lines to say one thing. The subheadline should add new information, not echo.

**Stock visuals.** The team high-fiving. The abstract purple gradient sphere. The 3D floating laptop. None of these tell me what the product does. The best hero visuals either show the product (a real dashboard screenshot) or show the outcome (a result, a metric, something concrete). If your visual could be on any SaaS homepage, it's not working hard enough.

**Two CTAs fighting each other.** Every button above the fold that isn't your primary CTA is competition for attention. Pick one action and commit to it.

**No social proof above the fold.** Even one line. Even a single number. Something that tells the visitor that other people have trusted this and it worked. For more detail on the [common hero section mistakes](https://humbldesign.io/blog-posts/how-to-fix-your-hero-section-and-increase-website-signups) that compound these issues, see the full breakdown.

## Before and after teardown

Here's the same SaaS product, same page, before and after applying this framework.

**Before:**

- Headline: *Streamline your team's workflow with AI-powered tools*
- Subheadline: *Our platform helps modern teams collaborate better and work smarter*
- CTA: *Get started*
- Social proof: none above the fold

**After:**

- Headline: *Your engineers are losing 3 hours a day to status updates*
- Subheadline: *[Product] fixes it. Seed-stage SaaS teams connect in 2 minutes. No process overhaul required.*
- CTA: *Start free — no card required*
- Social proof: *Trusted by 130+ engineering teams* + 3 company logos

Same product. Same layout. Completely different first impression.

The after version answers all three questions in under 5 seconds: what is this (fixes status update overhead), who is it for (seed-stage SaaS teams), why should I care (3 hours a day is a lot to lose).

## The 5-minute audit you can run right now

Open your landing page. Cover the logo. Give it to someone who doesn't know your product and ask them to explain what it does in one sentence.

If they can't, your hero is broken.

If they can but they describe a feature instead of an outcome, your headline needs rewriting.

If they get it but they're not sure who it's for, your subheadline needs specificity.

That's the audit. Five minutes. Most founders skip it. Do it today before you spend another dollar on ads.

## FAQ

**What should a SaaS hero section include?**
A strong SaaS hero section needs four things working together: a headline that leads with outcome (not feature), a subheadline that names the audience and mechanism, one specific CTA, and social proof placed as close to the CTA as possible. Remove any of these and the hero starts losing conversions.

**How long should a SaaS hero section headline be?**
Short enough to read in under 2 seconds. Aim for 6-12 words. The goal is clarity, not cleverness. If it takes more than one read to understand, it's too long or too vague.

**What's the best CTA copy for a SaaS landing page?**
Avoid generic copy like "Get started" or "Learn more." Name what happens after the click: "Start free trial," "Book a 15-min demo," or "Get your free audit." The more specific the action, the more confidence it gives the visitor to click.

**How do I add social proof if I have no customers yet?**
You don't need 50 logos. Use any signal that reduces perceived risk: a waitlist number, a quote from a beta user, a press mention, advisor credibility, or even the founder's background ("built by ex-Stripe and Notion alumni"). Early-stage social proof is about trust, not volume.

**How do I know if my hero section is working?**
Cover your logo and ask someone unfamiliar with your product to explain what it does in one sentence. If they describe a feature instead of an outcome, or can't tell who it's for, the hero needs work.
